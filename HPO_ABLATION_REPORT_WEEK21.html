Step 1 — Optuna HPO study

Study configuration

  • Search instance: ft06 (JSSP, 6×6, reference optimum C* = 55)
  • Fidelity: low-fidelity budget (100k timesteps per seed, 2 seeds per trial)
  • Evaluation metric: greedy-rollout makespan RPD (%) on ft06 at end of trial
  • Sampler: TPE multivariate (TPESampler(multivariate=True, seed=0))
  • Pruner: HyperbandPruner (checkpoint every 50k steps, prune on rolling-100-episode RPD)
  • Trials run: 9 completed or attempted (5 complete, 4 pruned); 1 additional trial was in RUNNING state when the study was interrupted (excluded from analysis)
  • Storage: experiments/results/optuna_ft06.db (SQLite, reproducible)

Justification of each parameter relative to the default Week 18 config:

param HPO winner Week 18 default direction & interpretation
learning_rate 1.06×10⁻⁴ 2×10⁻⁴ ~2× lower. Confirms the HPO spec's "lower LR prevents premature commitment" diagnosis.
num_layers 4 3 One more GIN message-passing round. The ft06 disjunctive graph has diameter ≤ 3, so 4 rounds is borderline over-smoothing, but both seeds still hit optimum, suggesting it is fine at this size.
hidden_dim 96 64 50% wider. Provides additional representational capacity without hitting the 128 that Trial 8 explored.
clip_range 0.25 0.20 Slightly larger PPO trust region. Together with the lower LR, this is not contradictory: a wider clip allows larger parameter moves when the gradient genuinely warrants them, while the lower LR keeps gradient magnitudes small.
ent_coef 0.0177 → 0.0041 over 25% of budget 0.01 (fixed) Starts with more exploration than the fixed default and decays quickly (in the first quarter of training). This schedule addresses the entropy-collapse pathology: high initial entropy when the policy is untrained, fast decay once a good policy region is found.

Tie-break note: Trial 1 (lr=2.1×10⁻⁴, 3 layers, hidden_dim=64, clip=0.15) also achieved 0.0% RPD on both seeds. That configuration is closer to the original Week 18 defaults and would be the more parameter-efficient choice for curriculum stages where compute is the binding constraint. For the ablation study (Step 2) and final curriculum (Step 3), Trial 0's configuration is used as the Optuna winner (Optuna's tie-breaking rule), but Trial 1 is flagged as a strong alternative.

Parameter importance

The parameter importance plot generated by optuna.visualization.matplotlib.plot_param_importances is saved at: experiments/results/hpo_param_importance_ft06_smoke.png

With only 9 trials (5 complete, 4 pruned), the importance estimates have wide confidence intervals and should be read as directional, not definitive:

  • ent_coef_start and decay_fraction show the highest apparent importance: the entropy-schedule design dominates the search. Trials with a moderate ent_coef_start (0.01–0.02) and fast decay (decay_fraction ≤ 0.30) consistently performed best.
  • learning_rate is the second-most important axis: all 5 complete trials with good performance had lr in [1.0×10⁻⁴, 2.2×10⁻⁴]. The two trials with lr ≤ 1.3×10⁻⁵ performed poorly (Trials 2 and 5), confirming the lower-bound of the search range is too conservative for ft06.
  • num_layers and hidden_dim showed lower apparent importance: ft06 is small enough that the policy reaches optimum across a wide range (2 to 4 layers, 64 to 128 dim).
  • clip_range had the least apparent importance among the five axes.

Caveat: Honest importance plots require ≥ 20 completed trials for stable Fanova estimates. The plot at 5 trials is mechanically correct but statistically underpowered. The full 40-trial study planned in HPO_TechSpec.md was not completed due to compute constraints; these importance rankings are preliminary.

Axis 1 — Encoder architecture: Friedman + Nemenyi test

Three encoder variants are compared: HeteroGIN (reference), HomoGIN (1a), HeteroGAT-hybrid (1b). Treating seeds as blocks and variants as treatments, the Friedman test is:

Friedman χ² = 2.0000, df = 2, p = 0.3679

The test is not significant (p >> 0.05). All three encoder architectures achieve statistically equivalent performance on ft06 at 400k steps with the HPO winner config.

Pairwise Wilcoxon (exact, two-sided, as post-hoc): - reference vs gat_hybrid: all differences = 0 → p = 1.0 (identical) - reference vs homogeneous: W = 0, p = 1.000 (one non-zero difference, both directions) - homogeneous vs gat_hybrid: W = 0, p = 1.000

Axis 1 conclusion: On ft06 at this compute budget, encoder architecture (GIN vs HomoGIN vs GAT-hybrid) does not significantly affect final policy quality. All three reach near-optimal or optimal performance. HeteroGIN is retained as the reference architecture on the grounds of theoretical motivation (heterogeneous graph captures machine–operation duality) and identical empirical performance, not empirical dominance. The GAT-hybrid's attention mechanism does not provide a measurable advantage on this small instance.

Important caveat: ft06 is a small, well-studied 6×6 instance. All variants converge to the global optimum within 400k steps; the test cannot discriminate architectures when both saturate at the ceiling. A more discriminating comparison would require harder instances (≥ 15×15) where optimality is not guaranteed — this is flagged as a future experiment.

At α = 0.05, the one-sided test is significant: dense reward produces significantly lower final RPD than sparse reward. The result is consistent across all 5 seeds.

Axis 2 conclusion: Dense reward (r_t = LB_{t-1} − LB_t, critical-path improvement) significantly outperforms sparse terminal reward at convergence quality. The margin is 7.27–9.09 RPD percentage points. The key mechanism is not learning speed (milestone timing is identical) but convergence quality: the sparse-reward policy occasionally discovers the optimum during training (best_training_makespan = 55 for 4/5 seeds) but cannot reliably reproduce it deterministically at evaluation time. Dense reward provides per-step gradient signal that shapes the policy toward consistent greedy-optimal behaviour.

This confirms the theoretical credit-assignment advantage of shaped reward for scheduling: with 36 decisions per episode and a sparse terminal signal, the policy receives only 1 non-zero reward per episode (vs 36 for dense), degrading the signal-to-noise ratio enough to prevent stable convergence at this budget.

Sparse reward mean (11.75%) is numerically lower than dense (16.17%) by 4.42 pp, but the difference does not reach significance. The sparse-reward distribution is wide (4.55%–20.42%, std = 5.74%) — seeds 1 and 2 achieved remarkably low RPD (7.20%, 4.55%) while seed 3 was the worst of any variant (20.42%).

Axis 2 conclusion (la16): The significant advantage of dense reward observed on ft06 does not transfer to la16. On harder instances where no variant converges to optimum, the credit-assignment advantage of shaped reward is not apparent at this sample size. Two interpretations are consistent with the data:

  1. Null effect at this difficulty: At 400k steps on la16, both reward modes reach similar RPD ranges and the shaped intermediates provide no marginal benefit because the policy is far from the optimal basin in all cases.
  2. Variance dominates: The sparse-reward policy has higher variance across seeds (std = 5.74% vs 4.84% for dense), masking any systematic effect. The same variance structure makes sparse reward look better in expectation here by chance.

The ft06 result (p = 0.031) was powered by zero variance in the dense arm (all seeds exactly 0%). That situation is unique to instances where dense-reward training reliably finds the global optimum; it cannot be assumed to generalise.

The reward-density finding is instance-dependent: dense reward is significantly superior when it enables reliable convergence to optimum (ft06), but not otherwise.
